My story begins with a restlessness that never allowed me to sit quietly inside boundaries built by other people. Even as a child in fourth and fifth standard, I did not want to stay confined inside the walls of a predictable routine. At that young age, I actually wrote an escape letter to my family, declaring that I was going to take debit cards, credit cards, and a mobile phone, and go live happily somewhere else in the world on my own terms. The letter was found before I ever made it out the door. I received beatings, and I watched my mother, my father, and my grandmother break down emotionally. That early moment taught me a lesson that has followed me ever since: my instinct is always to break free and explore the world firsthand, yet every step I take toward my own freedom carries an emotional weight for the family I love.

In sixth standard, my life was uprooted overnight when my father’s job forced us to move to a completely new state. I arrived in a place where I did not know a single word of the local language, surrounded by unfamiliar faces and swallowed by deep homesickness. Unable to adapt to the regional language, I chose Hindi as my bridge, hoping a universal language would help me survive—yet in the beginning, nothing worked. My exam scores dropped to zero, one, and two out of a hundred. Those early years in a strange place were the most isolating and challenging period of my childhood, and I became one of the most targeted kids in school. Still, I refused to break. Slowly, by observing people and adapting from the inside out, I learned the language, figured out how to live among strangers, and survived the turbulence of early teenage attractions and confusion.

By the start of tenth standard, that childhood restlessness returned as the single biggest turning point of my early life. For twenty-one straight days, I stopped going to school—even though my own mother was a teacher in that very same school. Every morning, I dressed in my school uniform and walked out the door as if heading to class. Once out of sight, I changed out of my uniform, boarded random trains and buses, and traveled across cities entirely on my own. Across those twenty-one days, I watched strangers on trains, felt their joys and their grief, cried, laughed, and observed how real life actually unfolds outside classroom walls. People called it truancy, and on the twenty-first day, my story caught up with me. As I was changing back into my uniform near my house to pretend I was returning from school, I walked straight into my principal, the school correspondent, and my class in-charge waiting outside my gate. I was beaten and scolded on the spot, and then came the moment burned permanently into my memory: standing in front of my father and mother, the principal declared that I did not deserve to study, that I had no wish to learn, and that I was worthless and unfit to do anything in that school.

Instead of destroying me, that public humiliation awakened something fierce inside me. I carried zero guilt for those twenty-one days because without them, I would never have understood life—and I made up my mind to prove that "worthless" label wrong on my own terms. I refused to ever memorize a single textbook paragraph. Even when teachers punished me for not reproducing bookish answers word-for-word, I insisted on understanding how every concept actually worked and writing every exam in my own words. At the end of tenth standard, I also experienced my first silent, one-sided infatuation—feelings I mistook for love, which fear kept locked inside my chest without ever telling her. Despite carrying that quiet weight and studying entirely through conceptual understanding, I scored 402 out of 500 in my tenth board exams.

When I entered eleventh and twelfth standard, the world shut down under COVID lockdowns, and regular physical schooling disappeared. Yet instead of slowing down, I pushed myself harder than ever. Working from home without daily classroom supervision, I began exploring technology on my own—experimenting with cybersecurity concepts, organizing school-level competitions, and serving as an NCC cadet at school . Still refusing to memorize textbook answers, I relied purely on conceptual clarity and scored 401 out of 500 in my twelfth board exams. The same school system that had once doubted me now saw something entirely different: the same persons and my parents and praised me, telling them that I was capable of achieving anything I set my mind to.

That validation ignited a new drive in me. I channeled that momentum into national entrance exams, clearing JEE Mains at the 89th percentile and qualifying for JEE Advanced on my very first attempt. Through that score, I earned a seat at the Central University of Jammu and Kashmir, alongside an Information Technology counseling seat in Tamil Nadu through Anna University. For a moment, it felt like I had finally conquered the academic scoreboard—until reality and family duty collided. As the only male child in a household with three sisters, I carried the emotional weight of my entire family's fears. My parents and grandmother were terrified of sending me near the Line of Control in Jammu and Kashmir, convinced that frequent border tensions and conflicts could cost them their only son. Out of love and responsibility toward them, I let go of the Central University seat I had earned.

At that exact same moment, at the end of twelfth grade, a second blow hit me from within. I found out that the girl from tenth standard—the one I had silently admired for years—had entered a relationship with someone who had the courage to tell her how he felt. My pain was not anger at her, nor was it the pain of rejection; it was pure, burning anger at myself. I kept asking myself what kind of person holds feelings inside for years without the courage to speak a single word. That quiet regret, combined with giving up the college seat I had worked so hard for, broke something inside me, and I slipped into bad habits like smoking and drinking just to numb the frustration.

I eventually joined engineering College after initially entering college through a managment seat tied to my grandmother’s spiritual vow. My first six months of college were chaotic and directionless. Still haunted by what I had given up, I reattempted JEE during my first year and scored in the 86th percentile, missing the Advanced cutoff, while my bad habits grew heavier. Everything shifted because of a conversation with my hostel warden. He shared his own story with me—how he had written the NEET medical entrance exam three times, failed every single time, shifted to engineering, and eventually built a fulfilling life as an engineering lecturer. He told me that failing repeatedly does not mean we are worthless; it means we are being redirected toward something bigger that is actually written for us.

His words flipped a switch in my mind. Starting in my second semester—the seventh month of college—I stopped trying to force myself into rigid expectations and decided to explore every domain from the inside out without judgment. Every single semester, I built at least two hands-on projects just to understand how real-world systems actually flow. I refused to stay inside one narrow lane: I dove into Cybersecurity, Internet of Things, Machine Learning, Robotics, Software Development, and Agentic AI. Along the way, I stepped forward as a leader and educator—becoming the elected President of the one of the famous technical chapter where I led over twenty team members and reached more than two hundred students, serving as an Robotics Student Coordinator instructing 150 students, and delivering practical workshops on Robotics, IoT, and Google AppSheet to over 135 students and employees.

My curiosity quickly translated into real-world results. I became a Smart India Hackathon 2024 National Finalist presenting a decentralized voting solution, won second place out of more than five hundred participants at the AML Hackathon, placed in the top five at Massathon, and authored a Scopus-indexed book chapter in blockchain and AI. To understand how actual industries operate, I stepped into the real world across multiple internships and roles—building no-code manufacturing automation workflows on Google AppSheet, researching and prototyping an iot research, coordinating campus placement assessments, and joining a it company in foreign and getting a tag of Software Developer. Yet by my third year, because I had explored so many domains instead of locking myself into one narrow corporate title, society kept confronting me with a single question: "What do you want to become?" By the end of my degree, I finally understood the truth—no single, repetitive corporate box was ever going to fit me, because my real strength was seeing how the entire system connects.

In the middle of all that noise and searching, love walked into my life—not as a distraction, but as the one chapter I have never regretted. She was my college senior, someone drawn to my curiosity in cybersecurity and the way my mind worked. What began as conversations around hackathons and domains slowly grew into a rare, judgment-free space where two people could actually see each other without masks. When she finally looked at me and asked, "Do you like me?", the old fear from tenth and twelfth standard rose up and froze the words on my tongue. For a split second, I was terrified of losing her—until I remembered the heavy regret of staying silent years ago and chose courage instead: "Yes, I like you." There was no grand proposal, no immediate verbal answer from her that day, only the quiet certainty that we belonged on the same road. Because true love isn't built in a single dramatic moment; it is built by walking side by side, learning each other’s flaws, and choosing to stay when the road gets rough.

Our journey together became everything young, fearless love is meant to be—bunking classes, catching buses just to catch a glimpse of each other, sneaking near her home, and talking until the sun came up. When she graduated a year ahead of me, she didn't walk away; she joined my college as a teaching assistant just to stay close, bringing me lunch while I finished my final year. We fought, we stumbled, and we went through storms that would have broken most people, yet every single conflict forged unbreakable rules between us: never go to sleep while the other is still hurting, never lose your identity or raise your voice in anger, never let outsiders step between us, and never use her past traumas as a weapon. To love someone deeply is to become a safe harbor where their past can never hurt them again.

Now, at twenty-one years old, I stand at the sharpest crossroads of my life, carrying the weight of expectations on every side. I work as a technical trainer College, teaching Python and DSA to final-year students for ₹35,000 a month—yet my family still measures me against traditional yardsticks and views me as successful. At the same time, Ammu’s family will only accept a government job for marriage, leaving me with a one-year window to make our future official. Over the last two years, I have sat through seventy competitive exams—from GATE, BARC, HPCL, and IOCL to banking and SSC—and just applied for my seventy-first with RRB NTPC. I have cleared cutoffs, stepped into interview rooms, and been turned away—sometimes because panels misread my unfiltered confidence as arrogance, sometimes because of reservation, and often because graduating at twenty made me the youngest person in the room, losing out on age tie-breakers despite scoring identical marks.

For two straight years, I was trapped between two worlds: unable to enjoy my present work because I was chasing a government tag for her family, and unable to crack the exams because my soul was never built for rote memorization. Watching those repeated setbacks slowly wore down Ammu’s faith, until she stopped bringing up the government job altogether—even though her true lifelong dream isn't a government office at all, but becoming a content creator and building a life vlogging together after marriage. Refusing to stand still, I took a leap two months ago and registered my own company, placing Ammu as the owner and CEO, already executing our fourth client project—a full-scale CRM—while trying to balance my teaching role, my physical health at 83 kg, and the creative dreams we both want to bring alive.

I am not a story of seventy failures; I am a twenty-one-year-old who refused to stop walking, building, and loving even when the whole world demanded a single label.

You do not need the world's permission to be who you already are; when you stop chasing everyone else's approval and build your own table, the very people who doubted you eventually pull up a chair.
